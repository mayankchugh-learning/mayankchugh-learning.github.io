# What Does an Enterprise Architect Actually Do? (20 Years, One Answer)

Ask three people what an Enterprise Architect does, and you'll get three different answers.

A developer will tell you it's someone who draws diagrams nobody reads. A CIO will tell you it's whoever owns the technology roadmap. A consultant will hand you a TOGAF slide with forty boxes on it and call it a day.

None of them are wrong, exactly. That's the problem. The title has drifted so far that even people inside the profession describe it differently depending on which corner of the job they've personally seen. So instead of adding a fourth vague answer to the pile, I want to give you a real one — built from twenty-plus years of getting this wrong before I got it right.

This post also marks something new: a second content pillar on this channel. If you've been here for the AI and RAG work, that's not going anywhere. This is the other half of my career — Enterprise Architecture, IT Governance, and M&A — finally getting its own home.

## Chapter One: Solving Inside the System

I started where most people in this field start — hands-on. In technical and solution delivery roles at Coforge and TCS, the job looked like this: someone hands you a specific business problem — a broken integration, two systems that won't talk to each other, a process that's too slow — and your job is to design and deliver the fix.

You're deep inside one project, one system, one deadline. That's Solution Architecture, even before I had a name for it. It's a legitimate, highly skilled discipline in its own right, and I'd argue every good Enterprise Architect should spend real time here first. You can't credibly decide what belongs in a portfolio if you've never had to actually build the thing.

## Chapter Two: Deciding What Should Exist At All

Then the scope changed. Moving into roles at Capgemini and Deloitte, I stopped being handed one problem to solve inside one system. Instead, the questions got bigger:

- Should this system even exist in three years?
- Do we build this, buy it, or retire it?
- How do fifteen different systems across a business fit together without turning into chaos?

That's a fundamentally different job. It's not about solving a given problem well — it's about deciding which problems are even worth building a system for in the first place.

I got to ask those questions across some very different worlds: aviation, on accounts including Cathay Pacific and British Airways; banking, with HSBC; and luxury retail, with CHANEL. Different industries, different regulatory pressure, completely different customers — but the architecture questions were structurally identical every single time. That consistency is actually the tell. If the same questions keep showing up regardless of industry, you're doing Enterprise Architecture, not Solution Architecture. The patterns travel. The code never does.

## The Core Distinction

Here's the definition, stripped all the way down:

**A Solution Architect solves a problem within a system.** You're given the "what" — build this, fix that integration — and your job is the "how." It's deep, technical, and bounded. On any given day, you know exactly which system you're working on.

**An Enterprise Architect decides which systems should exist at all, and how that decision connects to business strategy.** You're often *not* handed the "what." You're the one who has to figure it out, by asking what the business is actually trying to become in three to five years, and what portfolio of systems gets it there without duplicating capability or creating a mess nobody can unwind later. It's broader, less code-facing, and far more about trade-offs than technical elegance.

Put side by side:

| | Solution Architect | Enterprise Architect |
|---|---|---|
| Scope | One project / system | Whole portfolio |
| Question | "How do we build this?" | "Should this exist?" |
| Facing | Technical delivery | Business strategy |
| Horizon | This release | 3–5 years out |

Neither is more important than the other. They're different altitudes of the same profession.

## Where TOGAF Fits

There's a formal framework that puts language and structure around exactly this distinction — TOGAF. It's the closest thing this profession has to a shared vocabulary, and it's where a lot of the vague-sounding EA jargon you've probably encountered actually comes from. I'm not going to unpack it here; it deserves its own piece, and it's coming next in this series.
