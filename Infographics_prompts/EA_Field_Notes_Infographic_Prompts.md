# FIELD NOTES — Learning Series Infographic Prompts (18–25)

A companion series to the teaching prompts (1–17). Where those explain a framework, these show the person behind it: what twenty years actually taught, what's being learned right now, and what was learned the hard way.

**Why it's visually distinct.** The teaching series is authoritative — modules, tables, decision trees. This one is a notebook. Same navy and gold, but lighter structure, a visible series ribbon, and a first-person voice. Two visual languages tell a viewer at a glance which kind of post they're reading, which is what makes a series feel like a series rather than a backlog.

---

## Shared series template

Every prompt below inherits this. It's repeated in each prompt so they stay paste-standalone, but change it here first if you want to adjust the whole run.

- **Format:** 1200×1500 portrait for LinkedIn (portrait outperforms square in-feed and leaves room for the ribbon).
- **Series ribbon:** top-left corner, gold band, small navy caps: `FIELD NOTES · No. XX`.
- **Byline:** Mayank Chugh, Enterprise Architect · TOGAF 9.2 · IT Governance · M&A Integration, https://mayankchugh-learning.github.io/ — bottom of every post.
- **Palette:** navy `#1B2A4A`, gold `#C9A227`, off-white `#F5F6F8`. No photographs, no logos, no company brand marks anywhere.
- **Fixed three-part structure:** a headline claim → the evidence or story → the transferable lesson. Consistent placement across all eight so the series is recognisable while scrolling.

**Shorts adaptation.** For the 9:16 vertical cut of any prompt below, add this instruction: *"Produce a 1080×1920 vertical variant: keep the series ribbon, the headline, and the closing lesson only. Drop the evidence panel and all supporting tables — those go in the spoken narration. Type size no smaller than 48px."* A Short has roughly three seconds to land one idea; the LinkedIn density will kill it.

## 22. The Migration Worked. The Operating Model Didn't.

*Mode: learned the hard way. Grounded in the 1.3 brief, kept generalised for confidentiality.*

```
Create a dark navy portrait infographic (1200x1500) in the FIELD NOTES series style. Top-left gold ribbon: "FIELD NOTES · No. 22". Navy #1B2A4A, gold #C9A227, off-white #F5F6F8. Notebook aesthetic. No photographs, no logos, no client names.

HEADLINE (two lines): "The cutover was clean." / "The Monday after wasn't." with the second line in gold.

MIDDLE — SPLIT PANEL, side by side:
Left, headed "WHAT WE DELIVERED" with green check markers: target architecture defined; transition sequenced; data migrated with integrity intact; cutover on schedule; no downtime to live operations.
Right, headed "WHAT WE HADN'T DEFINED" with amber markers: who owns the new reporting layer; who approves a change to it; who gets called when it breaks at 3am; which team's budget it comes out of.

CONSEQUENCE band (full width, amber-bordered, beneath the split): "Post-go-live, issues bounced between teams for weeks. Not because anything was broken — because nobody was sure whose it was. The architecture was right. The organisation around it hadn't been designed."

CLOSING LESSON (gold-bordered): "A target architecture answers what systems will exist. A target operating model answers who runs them on Monday. Delivering the first without the second is a technically successful project that still fails the people using it. Define the operating model before Day-1, not during the first incident."

## 23. Thirteen Years, One Client

*Mode: twenty-year lessons. Depth as a credential — an unusual angle in a field that rewards breadth.*

```
Create a dark navy portrait infographic (1200x1500) in the FIELD NOTES series style. Top-left gold ribbon: "FIELD NOTES · No. 23". Navy #1B2A4A, gold #C9A227, off-white #F5F6F8. Notebook aesthetic. No photographs, no logos, no client names.

HEADLINE (two lines): "Thirteen years as one client's technical interface." / "Here's what only time teaches." with the second line in gold.

MIDDLE — FOUR LESSONS, numbered, each a short heading with two lines beneath:

1. YOU OUTLIVE YOUR OWN DECISIONS. Long enough on one account and you meet your past architecture choices again as someone else's constraint. It changes what you're willing to defer.

2. RELITIGATION IS THE REAL COST. A decision without a documented rationale gets reopened every time the stakeholders change. Writing down why is cheaper than arguing it a third time.

3. TRUST IS THE ACTUAL DELIVERABLE. Being the person who reports bad news early, accurately, and with a next step is worth more over a decade than any single successful delivery.

4. DEPENDENCY MAPPING IS A HABIT, NOT A DOCUMENT. Coordinating across onshore and offshore teams means the sequence lives in your head before it ever reaches a diagram — and the diagram is how you check the head.

CLOSING LESSON (gold-bordered): "The industry rewards breadth on a CV. Depth is what teaches you which decisions you'll still be living with in five years — and that's the judgement nobody can shortcut."

## Two prompts I did not write

You asked for all three modes, and the "learned the hard way" mode is short by two. I've written the two that are documented — the underclaiming correction (No. 20) and the operating-model gap (No. 22). A third and fourth would need to be invented, and a fabricated failure attributed to you is the one defect that would do real damage on a channel built around accuracy.

If you want them, send me either:
- **an architecture decision you'd make differently today** — what the situation was, what you chose, what actually happened. This is also the missing input for video 5.2, so it does double duty.
- **a stakeholder or governance call that went wrong** — an escalation handled badly, a decision that got relitigated, a risk you flagged too late or too quietly.

Two or three sentences each is enough; I'll build the prompt around it.

## Posting notes

**Sequence.** Lead with No. 18 — it sets the honesty register, and every post after it is read in that light. Follow with No. 21, which is pure evidence, so the series establishes credibility before it starts discussing limits. Then No. 20, which is the one most likely to travel. Hold No. 24 until the series has a few posts behind it; published cold, a capability map reads as self-deprecation, but published after 21 and 23 it reads as confidence.

**Interleave with the teaching series** rather than running eight Field Notes consecutively. The two formats reinforce each other — a framework explainer followed by the person who's used it is a stronger pair than either alone.

**No. 25 is reusable.** Refresh the three stacks each quarter and repost. It's the cheapest recurring content in the whole series and it quietly demonstrates momentum.
